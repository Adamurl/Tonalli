## Full English translation

### Printed page 196

In the Florentine Codex there is a relationship between the beginnings of some trecenas and the days on which Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the bright star, appears on the horizon. This relationship is not at all clear concerning the influence detected in people's attitudes; it is treated very superficially and has caused some confusion among those who have wished to study it literally. Eduard Seler confused some plates of the Borgia Codex with movements relating to Venus, taking passages from Book IV of the Florentine Codex in which this figure aims his rays at human beings whom, according to Sahagún's informants and the Chimalpopoca Codex, he attacks with good or bad fortune, his effects varying according to the beginning of the trecena on which he appeared on the horizon. It is not sufficiently understandable why, naively or out of ignorance, they attribute to him good or bad fortune or supposed anger against human beings according to their age or social condition, or by setting the natural elements against them.

Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli.

On page 51 of the Annals of Cuauhtitlan it says:

> ...they knew under which sign he appeared and whenever he shone, he shot his rays at them and showed them his anger.

Sahagún, in Book I, says that if he appeared on the first day of the first trecena, One Crocodilian, he wounded old men and old women. If he appeared on One Jaguar, One Deer, or One Flower, he shot arrows at the young boys. If he appeared on the horizon on One Reed, he shot arrows at the rain so it would not fall; if he appeared on One Movement, he shot arrows at the young men and the girls; and if he appeared on One Water, they would suffer drought.

Glyphs of the Morning Star.

The cycles of Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli as Morning Star and Xolotl as Evening Star are fundamental to calendrical calculations. This heavenly body, with its synodic time, regulates the calculations generated by the Sun and Moon as observed from Earth. Neither Sahagún, Diego Durán, nor any other of the early religious chroniclers ever imagined that these calculations form Ilhuicamatiliztli, knowledge of the sky. Ilhuicamatiliztli or ilhuicaxicalmatiliztli are words you will never find written in the sources, but which are common among Indigenous Nahuas.

From the viewpoint of the observers of the sky in antiquity, the great celestial vault resembles an enormous inverted bowl filled with points of light.

Undoubtedly, all their observations were made from Earth; therefore, it is truly the center from which the movements of all the celestial bodies visible in the sky are perceived.

### Printed page 197

#### Knowledge of the sky (Ilhuicamatiliztli)

> What is it, a blue-green bowl filled with the popcorn they call momochitl?

This saying from Book VI of the Florentine Codex refers to the sky we see strewn with stars.

The mathematical combination of the cycles of Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, Venus, with the cycles of Tonatiuh, the Sun, and Metztli, the Moon, forms a very well-known symbol called a quintiduo or quincunx. It is a glyph generally formed by a square and five points which, to give it a name, we will call Ilhuicamacuilli, the number five of the sky, so that the reader may continue to become familiar with the Indigenous language and thought. The great bowl of the sky is filled with countless points of light of different sizes and appearances. Most resemble little eyes that continually blink, holding a fixed position in relation to other stars, forming figures in the sky as a group, such as Citlalcolohtli, which has the shape of a scorpion, or Mamalhuaztli, which resembles the pieces of wood or spinning sticks with which the New Fire was kindled in years with the number and sign Ome Acatl, Two Reed, every fifty-two years, beginning the Earth's bioenergetic cycles governed by Omacatl Tezcatlipoca.

Colohtli.

Mamalhuaztli.

There are other points of light that move very rapidly, leaving behind a trail like floating hair. They take a long time to be seen again by human beings, and it is said that few of them exist. They are known as Tzoncitlaltin because of their tail of light resembling Xochiquetzalli's hair.

There are other luminous points that break away from the sky toward Earth, as though deliberately letting themselves fall, quickly fading in the blackness of night. These are called Citlalhuetzi, and it is common to hear their song in the whistle of their fall in the night sky.

### Printed page 198

In the Celestial Bowl, a great band is clearly defined, beginning in Tlahuiztlanpa, the place of light, crossing the sky to Cihuatlanpa, the place of women, dividing the sky into two halves. This celestial band is Ilhuicaohtli, the path followed by the heavenly bodies of steady light, those that do not blink, a band that also cuts in two the Cloud Serpent called Mixcoatl, apparently made from the dust of the stars. Citlalohtli is another name for Ilhuicaohtli, constantly observed by human beings, for within it move the heavenly bodies that guide them in governing their daily lives. Another name by which it is known in the Sierra of Hidalgo is Citlaltlachtli, the playing field of the heavenly bodies, in which their movements are bounded by the Ecliptic.

By moving in recurring cycles and constant rhythms, they served the ancestors of many generations in creating astronomical records in their time tables.

The Sun, Tonatiuh, and Metztli, the Moon, move along this path, each with its own cycles and its own sequence.

One of the heavenly bodies moving along this path has such brilliant luminosity that its nearness or distance can be appreciated with the naked eye through the intensity of the light, as though it were a mirror reflecting the light of the Sun. When its presence in the sky is as the morning star before the Sun rises, its name is Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the Lord of Dawn, which is when it advances ahead of the Sun. When it moves after the Sun, we see it in the afternoons and at nightfall; then it is called Xolotl, the twin or companion.

As the principal elements of the count of time called Cauhpohualli or Tlaxiuhpohualli, the cycles of Tonatiuh, Metztli, and Xolotl-Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli were chiefly recorded.

The cosmic instant at which the periods of these three heavenly bodies join served those who keep the Counts of Time, the Tonalpouhque of the most remote antiquity, to begin counting the cycles, taking as their measure the rhythm of the Sun in the Tlapohualxihuitl with respect to Earth. From Earth, Tonatiuh is observed occupying the same place in the sky after an average of three hundred sixty-five and one-quarter days has passed. The actual duration of the Sun's cycles was discussed at length in the first part of this book.

When the influence of Venus helps flowering.

If we had the curiosity to record the serpentine movement of the heavenly bodies that move in the celestial band, we would see that Xolotl-Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli has its own rhythms, for this large and luminous heavenly body follows its course along an

### Printed page 199

imaginary line; after five periods have passed, in each of which it has occupied the same place in the sky, it has traced a four-petaled flower, considering Earth as the point of observation.

If, hypothetically, we made the record from a point outside our planet, such as the fixed northern star called Citlalmictlanpa, which serves as the axis for the rotation of the entire Celestial Bowl, the resulting record traces a five-petaled flower after the five cycles of Xolotl-Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli have passed. These records gave rise to a glyph of great importance in the Astronomy of Anahuac, which, as we mentioned earlier, is a square with five points inscribed within it, with one point as the central element. Placed at the four corners of the square, these three principal heavenly bodies have a precisely defined value:

> Tonatiuh: each time it again occupies the same place in space is taken as one complete solar period.
>
> Metztli, the Moon, has its own period, which is very noticeable because its changes are very evident. As it fills with light until it is complete, this is because it is diametrically opposite Tonatiuh, the Sun. Then it begins to wane, shrinking in size until it disappears completely, thus beginning a new lunar cycle.
>
> Xolotl-Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli: each time it completes five cycles as seen from Earth, exactly eight years, chicueyi xihuitl, have passed on Earth.

All these records are condensed in the glyph inscribed on numerous astronomical monuments throughout the territory of Anahuac, with some variations but without losing the original design.

The mathematical relationship of the five-point symbol to a period of eight years is unquestionable:

Every eight years, five times twenty periods of Metztli pass, counted from its birth until it again disappears from our sight. The average calculation contained in Ilhuicamacuilli is two thousand nine hundred twenty-two elapsed days.

Every eight years, Xolotl-Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli completes five of its cycles.

Every eight years, Tonatiuh completes eight of its cycles.

From these three principal records it is deduced that Ilhuicamacuilli represents eight elapsed years and shows us beyond doubt that the mathematical relationship of these heavenly bodies' cycles is the foundation for the count of time.

The principal cycles composing Tlaxiuhpohualli or Cauhpohualli are:

- 584 days and a small fraction for the morning and evening star.
- 365 and one-quarter days for the Sun in relation to Earth.
- 29 days and a fraction for one lunar period on average.

#### Mathematical verification

The average cycle for one complete lunation, based on the calculation of 1300 lunations occurring in 104 years, is set at 29 days, 5 hours, 16 minutes, and 48 seconds, which, expressed in the decimal system, averages 29.22 days.

The time taken by the Morning and Evening Star to return to the same place in the sky is 584 days, 9 hours, and 36 minutes; this period converted to decimals is 584.40 days.

For the Sun we have an average of 365 days and 6 hours, or 365.25 days in decimal form. The actual time is 365 days, 5 hours, 49 minutes, and 46 seconds.

For all the reasons set out above, we believe that the basic counts of astronomical mathematics are condensed in the glyph of Ilhuicamacuilli, with the primordial counts that served the ancestors of Anahuac in creating the Cauhpohualli in their calculations of time. In comparison with the calendars of most cultures throughout the world, it has proved to be the most exact system created by human intelligence.

### Printed page 201

In six and one-half quincunxes, 52 years are inscribed: one Xiuhmolpilli.

In thirteen squares, 104 years are inscribed: one Huehuetiliztli.

In 1979, the UNAM Institute of Astronomy was asked for the average duration of lunar cycles over 104 years.

The result over such a long span of time was, on average, 1300 cycles from New Moon to New Moon of 29.22 days.

The request was made on the grounds that, because of the imperceptible wobble of Earth's axis, there is a noticeable variation that causes lunar cycles to fluctuate between 28 and 30 days. Taking into account that the complete cycle of the peoples of high culture in Anahuac was 104 years, we wished thereby to ascertain why the period called Huehuetiliztli was so important to the ancient Tonalpouhque:

> Exactly 37,986 days in 104 years make up a Huehuetiliztli, translated as an old age.
>
> 18,993 days form a Xiuhmolpilli, a bundle of 52 years.
>
> 37,986 days divided by 1300 lunations are 29.22 days.
>
> 18,993 days divided by 650 lunations are 29.22 days.
>
> The average cycle of Venus Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli is 584.4 days.
>
> Five cycles of this heavenly body are 2922 days.
>
> 18,993 days divided by 5884.4 are 32 and one-half cycles.
>
> 37,986 days divided by 584.4 are 65 complete cycles of this heavenly body.
>
> The average cycle of the Sun is 365.25 days, one solar year.
>
> Eight years are 2922 days.
>
> 365.25 days multiplied by 8 years are 2922 days.
>
> 18,993 days divided by 52 years are 365.25 days.
>
> 37,986 days divided by 104 years are 365.25 days.

We finish this work by summarizing the practical usefulness of the calendrical system, adapting the information gathered in this book to be applied now, in our present lives, astonishing us still more as we discover that this teaching is for all times, always current and active. Knowledge of all our possibilities gives us coherence, rationality, and sensitivity to understand ourselves better, accepting ourselves with our successes and limitations through the positive and negative attributes inherent in each of us. By being conscious of them, we are in a position to encourage and increase those that are positive, always seeking harmony for daily coexistence.

That is the purpose of the counts of time and destiny created by our ancestors.
