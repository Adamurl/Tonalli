### Printed page 139

#### The importance of the meaning of numbers

We reiterate once again at the beginning of this chapter that the numbers accompanying the twenty signs and the four year bearers are thirteen. We sensed that each must have its own characteristics, so that each calendrical combination of sign and number would give an individual reading. The combination of the two factors, 20 signs and 13 numbers, would give us 260 different results.

For those of us who took part in this work in the 1980s, between 1983 and 1989, an incidental comment made years earlier, around 1974, by Dr. Paul Kirchhoff to Dr. Sergio Carmona Ríos, who was interested in pre-Hispanic medicine, was encouraging. It referred to paragraphs numbered 124 and 223 of the work Xiutlapoualli, belonging to Tezcacoacatl Cotzatzin, son of Apanecateuctli Xiuhcozcatl, published in 1976 as Toltec-Chichimec History. In the preface to that edition it says:

> ...Regarding calendars, we wish to mention that, as occurs in many sources, numerals associated with day names appear in personal names, and thus we have 2-Wind, 1-Serpent, 2-Death, and so forth. But in our source it is striking that in paragraph 223 there is a Chichimec person called Ce Yaotl, 1-War, and in paragraph 124 a Toltec-Chichimec called Chicnauc homitl, 9-Bone, is mentioned. This may imply that war and bone are day names we did not know. Perhaps the name bone is synonymous with miquiztli, death, and perhaps 1-War is the same as 1-Flint, since this is the calendrical name of Uitzilopochtli and Camaxtle, gods of war.

Apart from the concern that sources might contain day names other than the usual ones, the comment to Dr. Carmona concerned the special importance each number must have.

He said that in the Tecamachalco area in the state of Puebla he had heard it mentioned that in ancient times the numbers had different characteristics.

If a number had a value and a characteristic applicable to the accompanying day sign, it must also be applicable to each sign of the four year bearers.

### Printed page 140

In Man-God, Alfredo López Austin mentions the importance of combinations in relation to certain mythological figures. The tecpatl sign with the numeral 1 could not be the same, for example Ce tecpatl as the calendrical name of Huitzilopochtli, as Dr. Kirchhoff mentions, as with the number 4, Nahui tecpatl, the name of Tecuziztecatl and Tlazolteotl in some sources. With regard to certain important events, López Austin mentions a proposal by Dr. Alfonso Caso concerning the Mexica interest in relating dates important to them with the most prominent figures of their past:

> In a work by Alfonso Caso, The Eagle and the Prickly Pear Cactus, he said that the founding of Mexico-Tenochtitlan and, 52 years later, the appointment of the first tlatoani, Acamapichtli, corresponded to ce tecpatl years, the date consecrated to Huitzilopochtli. The Mexica had left Aztlan in 1064, an original ce tecpatl year, a reason that makes Caso's proposition more attractive... he extended it to the Toltecs who had left their city in ce acatl, which corresponded to the name of Quetzalcoatl.

Kirchhoff's personal comment awakened greater curiosity in us when López Austin mentioned another comment by Dr. Kirchhoff recorded in Toltec-Chichimec History, emphasizing the argument for the value of the symbolism of numbers:

> The influence of ritual numbers, not only upon the thought but upon the acts and social institutions of the Mesoamerican Indians, is an indisputable fact. Without recognizing it, it will never be possible for us to understand the complexities of their life and culture.

Our task was beginning to take shape. It consisted of beginning a search for data concerning numbers, starting in the area from which the manuscript belonging to Tezcacoacatl Cotzatzin came. Initially we chose an extensive area comprising Atlixco, Tecamachalco, Tehuacán, Acatlán, and Izúcar de Matamoros in the state of Puebla. During several vacation periods, students and some colleagues from towns in that area devoted their time to gathering data related to the subject occupying us. We note that, in addition to Toltec-Chichimec influence, a marked Mixtec influence remains latent in the area in question.

*Lady 1-Flower, on the day 2-Eagle of the year 12-Flint, speaks with Lord 1-Ocelot in a place between two snow-covered mountains. Plate 11, Nuttall Codex.*

### Printed page 142

In the synthesis we present here, containing the most representative data collected from that stage onward, we add, for the first four numbers, the information provided by the nahuatlahto Luis Flores Torres, a native of Ocuilixtlahuaca in the mountains of Guerrero, concerning the origin of their names. In it there is a clear relationship between these four numbers and the primary integration of the human being as the logical representative of a base-twenty system whose existence, when combined with the factor thirteen, serves to develop fully from that beginning until reaching other evolutionary stages of individual and community integration. With this basic knowledge, the peoples of antiquity attained their fully recognized states of high culture.

The synthesis also shows us the direct relationship that each numeral has with the sign of the day of birth. It includes another relationship, no less important, that the numbers have with thirteen accompanying birds and with thirteen figures called the Lords of the Days, tonal teteuctin, who confer other special aptitudes, increasing the overall view of an individual's psychological profile. In a separate chapter we will analyze the influence of the nine Lords of the Night, directly related to people's inner attitudes and individual introspection.

It is appropriate to clarify something about the thirteen flying companions. Since they are not documented in any of the sources written in the sixteenth century, there is no coherent identification. The Borgia Codex is the only pre-Hispanic amoxtli in which their relationship with the thirteen numerals can be consulted. Eduard Seler and Alfonso Caso identified the drawings on plate 71. In three post-Hispanic codices, the thirteen flying companions appear in the trecena tables, placed with their respective number and daytime companion.

We have taken the drawings on plate 71 of the Borgia as the basis for naming them, since it is the oldest version available to us, although identifying the fifth flying creature is very difficult because its pictograph on that plate is so deteriorated.

The three post-Hispanic codices are the Borbonicus Codex, the Tonalamatl of Aubin, and the Tudela Codex. In all three documents, the drawings have pictographic styles that bear little relationship to the styles of the ancient tlacuilos, producing understandable confusions. Such is the case with the edition of the Tonalamatl of Aubin sponsored by the government of Tlaxcala in 1981, in which they identify a little dove in the place of the falcon in the Borgia Codex. Number five may indeed be a raven instead of the obsidian eagle mentioned by Seler and Alfonso Caso, since, as we have already commented, its identification is difficult.

In the following table we have placed the thirteen numbers with their respective companions, one of which is a butterfly, the thirteen Lords of the Days who exert their influence only during the part of the day that has light, called tonal, and another thirteen figures who emerge from the beaks of the flying creatures in the Tonalamatl of Aubin, from the region of Tlaxcala.

Otherwise, the differences among these three documents are slight and concern form more than content. For example, for the flying companion of number one, while the Borgia Codex names the xiuhuitzitzilli, the turquoise-blue hummingbird, as the companion, the Tonalamatl of Aubin has iztac huitzitzilin, the white hummingbird. In the Borbonicus it is gray, nextic huitzitzilli.

Huitzitzitzilli. Borgia Codex.

Regarding the daytime companions, in number five it is evidently Tlazohteotl and not Tlazolteotl. In the amoxtin, the painted books, there is a notable conceptual difference between the one who is the weaver of life through the cycles of the Moon and who also generates everything with affection for her children, and that other one who gathers and transforms everything that has completed its life cycles. The difference is between tlazohtli, affection, and tlazolli, rubbish. Tlazohteotl is not mentioned in the written sources, which is why historians do not take that concept into account. But in the everyday language of Mexican-speaking communities, that is what they call the Earth, who provides everything, with a mother's affection, to human beings and to all living beings. When the Earth opens her arms to receive those who die, plants or animals that have completed

their cycles of existence, she places them beneath her skirt to be transformed into new forms of life. Then she is known as Tlazolteotl. In the sources and in history they pejoratively call her the eater of filth or the goddess of rubbish, forgetting that the idea of rubbish, like the idea of sin, arrived with dogmatic mentalities from across the sea. In the codices, the two concepts are perfectly differentiated. Tlazohteotl has a nose ornament and Moon decorations on her clothing, especially on her skirt. In her headdress is a band of cotton being spun with a spindle whorl inserted on a wooden spindle. That is why our people call the Earth the weaver of life. The difference with Tlazolteotl is that the latter wears an enormous butterfly-shaped bone on her skirt, or two crossed bones as a symbol of transmutation.

Tlazohteotl. Laud Codex.

For number six, the Tonalamatl of Aubin has Teoyaomiqui as the companion, whereas the Borbonicus Codex has Mictlantecuhtli. The meaning is the same: Teoyaomiqui is the essence of the dead warriors, and Mictlantecuhtli is the eternal renewer, lord of the place of rest and stillness together with his dual counterpart, Mictecacuihuatl. The flying companion of this number in the Borgia Codex and the Borbonicus Codex is tecolotl, the owl, whereas in the Aubin it is chicuahtli, the barn owl. Here we wish to point out that in several communities where we have been, it is not called chicuahtli but chicuauhtli. In the editions of the Borgia Codex and the Borbonicus Codex published by the Fondo de Cultura Económica, they call the fifth bird a little owl, and the one that is indisputably an eagle in the Borgia they call a kite, perhaps confusing it with a small hawk, which in the Mexican language would be cuixin and not tlohtli, which is the name of the falcon.

For number seven, Eduard Seler's commentary on the Borgia Codex mentions the ash-colored butterfly, or the color of tizatl, whereas the explanation of the Aubin calls it only a butterfly. It is the only flying creature that is not a bird and has a very special symbolism.

Tizapapalotl. Companion of numeral seven. Plate 71, Borgia Codex.

In number eleven, instead of Mictlantecuhtli, the Borbonicus has Chalmecatecuhtli, the lord of Chalma, as the daytime companion, without departing from the context of transformation.

Tlazolteotl. Laud Codex.
