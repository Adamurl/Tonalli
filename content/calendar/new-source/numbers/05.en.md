## Full English translation

### Printed page 153

#### Macuilli

Macuilli is number five.

In the Maya language it is ho.

In ancient mathematical writing it is represented by a bar formed from five dots. In hieroglyphic writing it is represented by the drawing of a hand. Its name in Nahuatl is macuilli, whose principal etymological root is ma, from maitl, hand. The other particle is cuilli. There are several possible translations for this second particle. If it derives from ocuilli, caterpillar or worm, it may refer to the fingers of the hand, which with great skill transform the materials the Earth provides us, transforming them by expressing feelings and emotions through our creativity.

Another possibility would be the word cuilia, which means to take something, in this case with the fingers of the hand, as dried medicinal plants are taken. The fingers are the means of measuring out the herbs, with appropriate doses consisting of what two fingers, three fingers, or five fingers can take, according to the case being treated.

### Printed page 154

Another possibility is cuilli, point, referring to the points that the five fingers leave on any receptive surface. Whatever the appropriate translation may be, all of them together suggest that macuilli represents the capacity for accomplishment through the manual skills inherited from the historical Toltecs, who knew how to express their knowledge and emotions through every possible material, making clay lie so that the figures appeared real, and likewise gold, feathers, and precious stones, showing the interior of their hearts in each of their works. Creative beings are healthy in body and spirit, centered beings. Metaphorically, they are the midpoint of a diagram formed by number four, with the central point in the exact middle.

In number five a new stage begins, in which the being is nourished by constant learning after having been formed in its primary stage.

The bird companion of this number is cacalotl, the raven, with shining feathers, although in the fifth position in the Borgia Codex, Eduard Seler and Alfonso Caso thought it was an itzcuauhtli, an obsidian eagle. If that were so, there would be two eagles in this series, since the eagle is the companion of number eight. Changing itzcuauhtli to cacalotl proved somewhat difficult because we had no references in Indigenous thought to how ravens behave, with characteristics applicable to people's conduct.

We began by analyzing the popular tales and sayings that refer to them, for example the saying, "Raise ravens and they will peck out your eyes," about those who are ungrateful to people who help them selflessly. "Mother raven" is what they call someone who takes pride in her children and sees them as exceedingly beautiful even when they are not really good-looking. This extends to the works people produce, which for their authors are like true children. For people who are always fearful, the raven's caw is a sign of ill omen. Spanish authors such as Miguel de Unamuno mention these assertions, born in ancient European traditions and brought to our continent in the customs that arrived in the sixteenth century, taking traditional root from the colonial period onward. But this does not mean that they are connected with the thought of those who wrote our painted books. To avoid judgments foreign to Indigenous thought, we began by observing ravens' behavior in their natural surroundings. In the mountains of Tapalpa and Ameca, in the state of Jalisco, large cacalotl with shining feathers are abundant. They are strong-looking and have large feathers compared with thrushes and zanales. Everything that shines attracts their attention. They pick it up and store it among rocks, taking care that no one approaches their treasures. They live in pairs, and the females take great care of their young.

### Printed page 155

The view that the Cuzcanes and the Purépechas of Jalisco have of crows is that they are birds of beautiful appearance because of their black, shining plumage, and are conceited because of it. They have a loud voice, for their cawing frightens other animals. They like to keep what is beautiful and valuable to them. They are very responsible in caring for their loved ones. We have gone into greater detail in the description of the *cacalotl* than in those of other birds because we did not wish to use familiar popular judgments that are foreign to the thinking of those who, in ancient times, saw in the *cacalotl* human attitudes in relation to the numeral five, some positive and others that must be struggled against, since from its beak in the *Tonalamatl de Aubin* emerges Yaotl, the enemy, who forms an important part of dark Tezcatlipoca.

**Macuilli, five.** It is manual skill, union, and centrality.

**Cacalotl, the crow.** These are people proud of what they possess, faithful in their relationships, somewhat vain, jealous of everything that concerns them, and very faithful in their relationships.

**Tlazohteotl.** Affection, protection, and responsibility toward those close to them. They are very loving and protective people.

**Yaotl.** It is the ability to assess extreme attitudes to avoid [illegible] conflicts.
