## Full English translation

### Printed page 131

#### Cozcacuauhtli

Cozcacuauhtli translates literally as collared eagle, but to date it has not been possible to identify it with any known species. Even in the testimonies gathered, we found no identification:

> The name cozacacuauhtli means collared eagle. We do not know what animal it is. The only one we know with a collar of feathers is the one we call aura. In the hot country, the zopilotes or nopos have bare heads and necks with a little collar of feathers. These birds feed on animals that die in the fields and mountains, cleaning Mother Earth of what is already dead. They eat everything, make use of everything. When they find their food they fly down in circles until reaching it.

*Cozcacuauhtli. Plate 58, Borgia Codex.*

In the chronicles they translate the name of this sign as turkey vulture, vulture, or buharro, always relating it to some kind of bird of prey.

In his history, Fray Diego Durán gives us a portrait of those born on this day:

> ...the cozcacuauhtli sign, which means buharro, signified and foretold for those born under it a long life, healthy and strong without illness, tall, stout, muscular, bald, discreet, men of great counsel and authority. He later adds that... they are wise, serious, quiet, prudent, eloquent, fond of and inclined to teaching and preaching, fond of giving good advice and rebuking.

*Stone of the Suns.*

It is also said that they are healthy people who know how to make the most of the opportunities presented to them, and are very skilled at finding practical uses for ideas, objects, tools, and all kinds of materials that others have discarded.

This sense of practicality is better perceived in a calendar from the Jalatlaco area, discussed by Dr. Jacinto de la Serna in the Treatise on Rites, Superstitions, and Sorceries. Instead of the cozcacuauhtli sign on this day, the sign is that of the stone grinding slab, the temetlatl, an indispensable instrument in Indigenous society:

> The sixteenth is Temetlatl, which is the mill in which maize is ground, and which to this day retains this name among us.
