- **B, PDF page 10** is a distorted duplicate of printed pages 104–105. Clear copies exist on PDF pages 2 and 11.

### How to read the English below

The English entries are **detailed, condensed translations of the available meaning-bearing passages**, organized under the source's own subsection pattern. They are not a diplomatic transcription or a sentence-for-sentence bilingual edition. Repetition is reduced; long quotations are translated in condensed prose; original headings and distinctive terms remain available for comparison.

The book's etymologies, historical reconstructions, birth-character descriptions, and accounts of cosmic energy are preserved **as the book's interpretations**. Translation does not establish their linguistic, historical, biological, or psychological accuracy. For example, the text makes claims about lunar effects on the mind and bodily fluids, activation of genes by Itzpapalotl, and ancient memories of prehistoric reptiles. These have not been independently substantiated here.

Throughout section 5, **“Tonalpohualli interpretation” records what the source attributes to people born under a sign**. It should not be read as a verified assessment of an individual. The source itself sometimes qualifies a reading by the other elements of the calendar, explicitly in the Cuetzpalin entry.

**Editorial additions** are the symbol associations, project slugs, compact theme labels, coverage warnings, and comparisons in sections 6–9. The project images are existing assets, **not glyph illustrations reproduced from these PDFs**. Their placement identifies which project symbol corresponds to each translated entry.

| Spanish or Nahuatl heading | English used here | Function in the source |

| Características de los signos de los días | Characteristics of the day signs | The main twenty-sign reference section |
| A sign name, such as Cipactli or Calli | The Nahuatl name, followed by an English symbol label | Name, imagery, natural referent, etymology, and symbolism |

| Regentes | Ruling figures / patrons | A paired association rather than a single figure |

“Ruling figure” translates the book's category without assuming that every named figure is a separate deity. The book frequently presents named figures as natural functions, complementary forces, forms of knowledge, or attained roles.

## 3. The thirteen numbers

**Source section:** *Características de los trece números* → *Tlapohualiztli / Números*.

**Location:** [A, PDF pages 1–4](scan.pdf#page=1), printed pages approximately 61–66.

### 3.1 Number-symbol index

The source depicts **one unit as an open circle** and **five units as a horizontal bar**. Thus 6–9 have one bar plus circles; 10 has two bars; 11–13 have two bars plus circles. In the printed illustrations, the circles sit above the bars.

The notation below describes the source illustrations; it is not a substitute for a final glyph asset.

| Number | Source name | English | Source symbol construction | Concise source theme |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Ce / cen / cem | One | 1 circle | Origin; unity; a whole made of parts |
| 2 | Ome | Two | 2 circles | Structure; symmetry; complementary duality |
| 3 | Yei / eyi | Three | 3 circles | Blood and water; life-bearing circulation |
| 4 | Nahui | Four | 4 circles | Embodiment; four directions; ordering forces |
| 5 | Macuilli | Five | 1 bar | Hands; making; artistic perception |
| 6 | Chicuace | Six | 1 circle + 1 bar | Introspection; internal integration |
| 7 | Chicome | Seven | 2 circles + 1 bar | Active intellect; inner transformation |
| 8 | Chicueyi | Eight | 3 circles + 1 bar | Inner development joined to physical growth |
| 9 | Chicnahui | Nine | 4 circles + 1 bar | Centrality among eight surrounding points |
| 10 | Matlactli | Ten | 2 bars | Two hands; complementary skill and balance |
| 11 | Matlactli huan ce | Eleven | 1 circle + 2 bars | A pause; lightness; expression and enjoyment |
| 12 | Matlactli huan ome | Twelve | 2 circles + 2 bars | Inner beauty; clarity; transparent thought and action |

### 3.2 One: Ce / cen / cem

**Source:** [A, PDF page 1](scan.pdf#page=1), opening number page, inferred printed page 61.

**English translation:** This is the first number in the count of destiny. It signifies the beginning and the origin. It also represents a unit, *ce*, or an entirety, *cen*: several elements brought together to make a whole.

The source illustrates this with *cemanahuac*, its conception of the Indigenous universe, and *cenyeliztli*, the family understood as a life-generating unit. A human being is likewise a whole composed of multiple elements, which the subsequent numbers develop.

**Association with the symbol:** A single circle expresses one unit; the interpretation extends that unit to a unified whole.

**Theme labels:** origin; beginning; unity; wholeness.

### 3.3 Two: Ome

**Source:** [A, PDF pages 1–2](scan.pdf#page=1), inferred printed page 61 and printed page 62.

**English translation:** The book derives the name from *omitl*, bone: the skeletal structure that supports vertebrate beings and gives human beings shape, strength, and firmness.

Symbolically, *ome* represents the dual essence that forms everything in existence, called *Omeyotl*. Feminine and masculine elements unite in *Ometeotl*, the dual generating principle, described as the principle of principles present in the essence of everything.

Two also represents symmetry throughout the universe, such as a butterfly's wings and the arrangement of human limbs and principal organs. The cosmic dual spirit of Ometeotl is presented as a generating and evolving essence present in all that exists.

**Association with the symbol:** Two circles correspond to paired elements and the source's complementary duality.

**Theme labels:** structure; support; symmetry; complementary forces.

**Editorial note:** The derivation from *omitl* is the book's explanation, not an independently checked linguistic conclusion.

### 3.4 Three: Yei / eyi

**Source:** [A, PDF page 2](scan.pdf#page=2), printed page 62.

**English translation:** The source links the name to the precious liquid flowing through countless channels inside the body. This liquid carries warmth and energy into the body's most hidden places and gives human beings and some animals the force that animates them. It calls this liquid *yeztli* or *eztli*: blood.

Blood symbolically combines water with inner heat. When courage and determination intensify that heat, we say that our blood boils; the text connects this with *atlachinolli*, “burned water.”

Three also concerns the sap-like fluid of plants and, more broadly, all the waters of nature: water moving horizontally through rivers into lakes, and water falling from above that the earth drinks to generate new life.

**Association with the symbol:** Three circles identify the numeral; the source's additional association is with circulation and life-bearing fluids.

**Theme labels:** blood; water; warmth; animation; circulation; nourishment.

### 3.5 Four: Nahui

**Source:** [A, PDF page 2](scan.pdf#page=2), printed page 63.

**English translation:** The book derives the name from *nacahui*, to cover with flesh or to embody. Four gives form to the human whole by covering its bony structure, *omitl*. It also contains the channels through which the precious liquid of life flows.

Four is presented as an elementary conception of the universe: the principal forces converge from each sacred direction. It brings together four basic elements, described as heat, wind, and water acting upon the generating earth.

The source also identifies four first children of Ometeotl: Yayauhqui, Tlatlauhqui, Huitzilopochtli, and Quetzalcoatl. In their cosmic function they represent dark, infinite time in which ordered wholes acquire coherent form. Their constant activity gives rise to life. They share the duality of Omeyocan and give time, coherence, movement, and life to eternal space.

**Association with the symbol:** Four circles; the corresponding interpretive structure is the four sacred directions and their ordering forces.

**Theme labels:** embodiment; directions; material form; cosmic order; life.

### 3.6 Five: Macuilli

**Source:** [A, PDF page 2](scan.pdf#page=2), printed page 63.

**English translation:** The book brings together *ocuilli*, a caterpillar or small worm, and *maitl*, hands. It describes the fingers as the little creatures on our hands that can transform the materials Mother Earth supplies.

Five represents human ability to accomplish things through the movement of the fingers. It is also the sensitivity of perception through which beauty is appreciated. Five is a first step toward centrality, artistic creativity, and the expression or sharing of a person's values, incorporating the elements represented by the preceding numbers.

**Association with the symbol:** One bar stands for five, corresponding here to the complete set of fingers of one hand.

**Theme labels:** hands; skill; making; perception; beauty; artistic creativity.

### 3.7 Six: Chicuace

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 64.

**English translation:** Six signifies the beginning of integration and inner transformation through introspection. It also means examining the philosophical principles of the preceding sequence, which together form an already constituted human being.

The book explains it through one, *ce*, being added or placed before the preceding elements, *chicu*: one finger from the other hand joins the already open hand.

**Association with the symbol:** One circle above one bar; one additional finger joins the completed five.

**Theme labels:** introspection; integration; self-examination; inner transformation.

### 3.8 Seven: Chicome

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 64.

**English translation:** Seven is the second step of inner transformation, arising from mental restlessness. This increasing activity awakens the human intellect and gives structure to what has already been accomplished.

It is active intellect combining with other aspects of life, which the source describes as governed by, and changing with, lunar influence.

**Association with the symbol:** Two circles above one bar.

**Theme labels:** active intellect; mental activity; structuring; inner change.

### 3.9 Eight: Chicueyi

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 64.

**English translation:** Eight is a third step in inner evolution as it joins physical development. The inner essence must gradually rise from the earliest conditions of an organically formed being, treated as a vessel in which the cosmic essence has been deposited.

The source relates this to the third finger of the other hand joining the already complete hand.

**Association with the symbol:** Three circles above one bar.

**Theme labels:** inner growth; physical development; a vessel of essence; gradual emergence.

### 3.10 Nine: Chicnahui

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed pages 64–65.

**English translation:** Nine is a second evolutionary phase for beings seeking to reach centrality. It is the center among eight points marking the world's four directions and four corners.

**Association with the symbol:** Four circles above one bar identify nine; the interpretive arrangement is a central point with eight surrounding positions.

**Theme labels:** center; centrality; orientation; integration of surrounding directions.

### 3.11 Ten: Matlactli

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 65.

**English translation:** Ten represents the hands brought together. It symbolizes reaching the halfway point of an initial realization of the twenty positions in the count of destiny.

Duality is present in our hands: the skill of one joins the balance supplied by the other.

**Association with the symbol:** Two bars; two completed sets of five.

**Theme labels:** two hands; cooperation; skill; balance; halfway to twenty.

### 3.12 Eleven: Matlactli huan ce

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 65.

**English translation:** Ten and one is a rest or pause in the process of transformation before continuing along the upward path of the spiral.

It means grace, lightness, and ease of expression. In nature it is splendor, color, and pleasure inviting leisure and enjoyment.

**Association with the symbol:** One circle above two bars.

**Theme labels:** rest; pause; grace; expression; color; enjoyment.

### 3.13 Twelve: Matlactli huan ome

**Source:** [A, PDF page 3](scan.pdf#page=3), printed page 65.

**English translation:** Ten and two represents the growth of inner beauty in essence and spirit. It is translucence and clarity: like water, these are qualities people should make visible in their thoughts and actions.

**Association with the symbol:** Two circles above two bars.

**Theme labels:** inner beauty; clarity; transparency; coherent thought and action.

### 3.14 Thirteen: Matlactli huan yei

**Source:** [A, PDF pages 3–4](scan.pdf#page=3), printed pages 65–66.

**English translation:** Thirteen is presented as a factor used to understand the cycles of moving celestial bodies in the *Jícara Celeste*, the celestial bowl, and the gestation cycles of many earthly beings. The text describes formation within the female vessel as thirteen times twenty days plus thirteen, from the depositing of the precious seed until the new being breathes *ihiyotl*, the breath of life, on emerging from the maternal womb.

Thirteen times twenty is the combination of possibilities of destiny: paths through which beings transform and learn to elevate themselves. In human life, thirteen becomes manifest when a person's life, undertakings, and achieved goals are interwoven with those of all living beings in a framework of respect for everything that exists.

One who possesses the essence of thirteen has already flowered and is ready to bear fruit. One through thirteen constitutes the first count of destiny, in which the preceding numbers gather into principles of action, inner knowledge, integration, introspection, centrality, and transparency.

The book calls thirteen the number of the universe's wisdom, understood through the ancient Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl traditions it associates with the world before Cuauhtémoc.

**Association with the symbol:** Three circles above two bars.

**Theme labels:** cycles; interdependence; respect; wisdom; flowering; fruitfulness; synthesis.

**Editorial note:** The source uses two related but distinct expressions: **13 × 20 = 260**, and a gestation expression of **13 × 20 + 13 = 273**. Preserve that distinction. Its gestation claim is part of the source's interpretation and is not a general medical rule.

## 5. Day-sign translations

### 5.1 Cipactli: crocodilian / primordial earth creature

**Project key:** `cipactli`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Cipactli image](../frontend/assets/icons/Cipactli.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF pages 4–5](scan.pdf#page=4), printed pages 67–69.  
**Source section:** *Características de los signos de los días* → *Cipactli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Cipactli is the first day sign of the Mexican calendar; the source says the calendar's months and years begin with it. Sixteenth-century chronicles translate its name as caiman, swordfish, fish, or serpent's head.

The book proposes an etymological analysis using *ce*, one; *icpac*, on or above; and the nominal ending *tli*, arriving at “upon the first.” It interprets Cipactli as the earliest earthly life registered in humanity's ancestral memory. It speculates that the sign represents great prehistoric reptiles that originated in water and emerged onto land, explaining why its depictions can resemble either fish or lizards.

The place of origin, Tamoanchan, is described as the place of turquoise-blue mist and rain, also called Xiuhayauhtitlan, where life originates. Early forms of life appear in swamps and marshes before emerging onto the land. **Cipactli represents this transmutation.**

Its fangs are represented by Tecpatl, flint, which the source interprets as the first light of knowledge recorded in human genetic memory: knowledge through which the formation of the universe, the world, and life came to be understood.

#### Regentes: Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl

**English translation:** The Lord and Lady of darts of heat and energy are the natural elements manifested in this sign. The book rejects translating their names as “lords of our flesh” and instead proposes an analysis of *tona-aca*: *tonalli*, heat, energy, destiny, or day according to context, and *acatl*, reed.

In its reading of pre-Hispanic manuscripts, reeds can be darts directed toward a specific productive purpose. It cites reeds in the Borgia Codex generating life, fire, and heat in the sky and on earth.

Tonacatecuhtli and Tonacacihuatl are expressions of Ometeotl, the dual generating principle active in everything. The source places them alongside other cosmic pairs, including Ometecuhtli and Omecihuatl, Ilhuicatecuhtli and Ilhuicacihuatl, Citlaltonac and Citlalin icue, Chalchiuhtlaltonac and Chalchiuhtlicue, Tlaltecuhtli and Coatlicue, and Mictlantecuhtli and Mictlancihuatl.

Together the pair symbolizes dual cosmic energy reaching the earth as heat. Combined with solar heat and energy and lunar influence, it generates terrestrial life. The book describes *tonalli-acatl* as darts of cosmic heat and energy arriving from the celestial bowl to fertilize Tonantzin, our mother Earth.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Cipactli is a beginning or a sign of leading people. Those born on a Cipactli day are described as enterprising, firm in their principles, and carrying substantial responsibility. They are people with roots, principles, and responsibilities in the world.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a crocodilian or composite primordial creature.
- **Source themes:** first life; origin; emergence from water; transformation; beginnings; responsibility.
- **Keep attributed:** the proposed etymology, prehistoric-reptile explanation, genetic-memory language, and universal month/year-start assertion.
- **Distinction:** the source gives a **paired** ruling association. Do not reduce it to Tonacatecuhtli alone when recording this book's account.

### 5.2 Ehecatl: wind

**Project key:** `ehecatl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Ehecatl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Ehecatl.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF pages 5–7](scan.pdf#page=5), printed pages 69–72.  
**Source subsections:** *Ehecatl* → *Regente: Quetzalcoatl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Ehecatl means air or wind. It is the breath of life, the first vital nourishment a person receives at birth and something no living person can do without. Its abstract sign is represented by a human head wearing a mask with a long projecting beak, suggesting the force of the blowing wind.

As part of Quetzalcoatl, understood here as the knowledge or science of life, wind transports clouds so they release water over cultivated fields. It is metaphorically called the sower: it sweeps the fields in preparation for rain and disperses seeds that clothe the earth with plants each year.

Air enables human existence and communication. It carries all sounds and is the means of expression of nature. Without it there would be no music or harmony in the sounds of water, birds, trees, or people. Wind also makes sounds of its own, from the light hiss of a breeze to the roar of a hurricane. The source suggests that destructive wind helped lead chroniclers to classify the sign as bad while overlooking its natural functions.

#### Regente: Quetzalcoatl

**English translation:** The source explains *quetzalli* as beautiful or precious and *coatl* as undulating energy. Knowing Quetzalcoatl's science means knowing the science of life. It describes high levels of knowledge among the *tlamatinimeh*, the wise, and recalls the Toltec ruler Ce Acatl Topiltzin Quetzalcoatl of Tollan.

Quetzalcoatl is presented as a concept integrating nature's elements in the animation of Tonantzin Coatlicue, Mother Earth. The serpent represents energy latent throughout nature: rain, wind, heat, and the earth itself. The book prefers “Beautiful Serpent” to “Feathered Serpent” for this interpretive concept.

Its table names the following elements or functions:

| Name in the source | English translation of the source's description |
| --- | --- |
| Ehecatl | Wind and the breath of life |
| Tlaloc | The liquid the earth drinks |
| Tonatiuh | The one who possesses heat |
| Mixtli | Clouds carried by Ehecatl to fertilize the earth |
| Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli | Lord of the Dawn |
| Xolotl | Evening Venus |
| Piltzintecuhtli | The child lord representing a function of the Sun |
| Zenteotl | The sole generator of sustenance |
| Xochipilli | The Sun's action upon plants |
| Macuilxochitl | Five Flower; human physical development through sport and games |
| Tecziztecatl | The one who carries the conch shell |

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Those born on an Ehecatl day are assigned a changeable character. Through reflection, this can be centered in creativity. Wind represents inspiration of the spirit, manifested as ideas that take material form in the arts. Other attributed qualities are ease of expression and harmony in singing.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** wind, represented through the beaked wind mask rather than an ordinary bird.
- **Source themes:** breath; communication; inspiration; sound; seed dispersal; rain-bearing clouds; creativity.
- The functions listed under Quetzalcoatl are a **nested conceptual table**, not eleven additional rulers of the day sign.

### 5.3 Calli: house / containing structure

**Project key:** `calli`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Calli image](../frontend/assets/icons/Calli.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF page 7](scan.pdf#page=7), printed pages 72–73.  
**Source subsections:** *Calli* → *Regente: Tepeyolohtli* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Calli is generally translated as house in the sense of a building or constructed structure, distinguished from *chantli*, a dwelling or home. It is something that contains, protects, or shelters.

The source illustrates the containing function with several compounds: *acalli*, a boat; *huacalli*, a slatted box holding something; *teocalli*, described here as a place where knowledge is kept; and *xicalli*, a bowl or vessel holding something in its center.

Calli is where each person should seek to know themselves inwardly. Each person is the vessel of their own essence and is solely responsible for it. Cincalco, another name the text gives for Temoanchan or Tamoanchan, is the primordial house: Mother Earth's great womb, where sacred, vital water brings all beings to birth and sustains them.

#### Regente: Tepeyolohtli

**English translation:** Tepeyolohtli means Heart of the Mountain. Its *nagual* or representation is a jaguar whose body is adorned with obsidian knives. A special conch-shell call imitates the feline's roar, linking it with mountain caves.

It symbolizes inward knowledge and belongs to the source's discussion of Tezcatlipoca, the smoke of the mirror through which past events can be seen and relived. The source links altered consciousness with Metztli, the Moon, whose full face bears the image of a rabbit. It locates disciplines of inner knowledge in places with caves, naming Chalman, Oztoticpac, and Oztotepec.

Its connection to Calli is the hidden interior of a house and the inward or subconscious part of ourselves that holds what we do not want others to know.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Those born under Calli are described as creative, home-loving, protective, and fond of family company. They plan ahead, are somewhat sedentary, and try to secure enough resources to avoid deprivation. The passage attributed to Diego Durán calls it a good sign and describes its people as peaceful and reserved.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a house or building.
- **Source themes:** containment; shelter; protection; inner knowledge; family; provision.
- Keep the **house sign** separate from the **jaguar representation of its ruler**. The ruler's animal does not change Calli into the Ocelotl sign.

### 5.4 Cuetzpalin / Cuetzpalli: lizard

**Project key:** `cuetzpalin`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Cuetzpalin image](../frontend/assets/icons/Cuetzpalin.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF page 8](scan.pdf#page=8), printed pages 74–75.  
**Source subsections:** *Cuetzpalin* → *Regente: Huehuecoyotl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

The name refers generically to small reptiles known as lizards. The source also discusses names for iguanas and tree lizards near water. It associates these reptiles with the fertility of the earth and human sexuality.

In the Borgia Codex, the text relates the sign to the male sexual organ of Xochipilli and Tezcatlipoca.

#### Regente: Huehuecoyotl

**English translation:** Huehuecoyotl, the old or elderly coyote, rules these days. He is depicted as a dancing coyote with an ear ornament shaped like a ray or arrow of time emerging from a circle and trapezoid. The text calls that geometric figure *tonameyotl*, a sign of pure energy produced here through dance.

The rattle staff, *chicahuaztli*, is described as fertilizing and as representing the pleasure of well-directed sexuality, enjoyed positively to preserve a couple's harmony, particularly in old age. Huehuecoyotl represents mature sexuality generated through elders' dancing in important ceremonies.

Cuetzpalli and Huehuecoyotl together represent serpentine energy received from nature and transformed inwardly into sexuality.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

The passage says those born on a Cuetzpalli day would have extensive lineage and descendants, **provided the other elements of the Tonalpohualli reinforce the sign**. Otherwise, it says they may be infertile despite strong sexual capacity.

The author suggests religious chroniclers called it a bad sign because they viewed human sexual instinct as diabolical.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a small lizard.
- **Source themes:** sexuality; fertility; pleasure; dance; mature partnership.
- The fertility and infertility statements are attributed divinatory claims, not reproductive guidance.
- The coyote is the ruler's representation, not the day-sign animal.

### 5.5 Coatl: serpent

**Project key:** `coatl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Coatl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Coatl.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF pages 8–9](scan.pdf#page=8), printed pages 75–77.  
**Source subsections:** *Coatl* → *Regentes: Chalchiuhtlicue y Chalchiuhtlahtonac* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Coatl means serpent, used here as a generic term for boas, vipers, and snakes. In the source's account of Anahuac cultures, it represents energy emanating from the elements and from all beings in nature, as well as the precious energy of life.

It also represents Mother Earth's fertility. Its energy interweaves in the serpent skirt of Tonantzin Coatlicue through related mathematical cycles that generate different species. A two-headed serpent symbolizes the fusion of two cosmic forces generating everything that exists.

Its adornments may be feathers, jewels, or rattles according to whether it represents terrestrial, aquatic, aerial, or cosmic qualities. Tezcatlipoca and Quetzalcoatl appear as two great serpents dividing Tlaltecuhtli and forming the earthly surface, *oxomoco*, and the atmospheric envelope, *cipatonal*, where heat and the elements needed for life accumulate. This also expresses opposing but complementary forces generating life.

#### Regentes: Chalchiuhtlicue and Chalchiuhtlahtonac

**English translation:** Coatl receives a double influence from the feminine and masculine essences of water, understood as part of cosmic Ometeotl. This vital liquid is where life formed before populating the terrestrial skirt of Tonantzin Coatlicue.

The source describes a naming ceremony in which a child was introduced to trees, stones, mountains, clouds, the Sun, wind, and earth. Finally the child received the two essences of water on parts of the body and tasted drops delivered with a reed by the elderly *ticitl*, the midwife. A passage attributed to Sahagún's informants invites the child to receive the water of life, father and mother together: clean, clear water that never sleeps, allowed to penetrate the heart.

In the age called Atonatiuh, Sun of Water, Chalchiuhtlicue ruled as flowing water. The author speculates about post-glacial meltwater filling cavities in the earth and humans leaving caves. It distinguishes this account from other traditions' universal flood and explicitly distinguishes **flowing water** from **rain falling from the sky**, which it assigns to a different natural function.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Those born on a Coatl day are described as generous or unattached to possessions, uninterested in hoarding, and inclined to change their personal appearance or their surroundings. The text attributes communication through thought with people on a similar wavelength and sensitivity in the skin to atmospheric changes.

It argues that European chroniclers called the sign harmful because they interpreted the serpent through the Christian association with original sin.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a serpent.
- **Source themes:** life energy; fertility; complementary forces; water; change; generosity.
- Preserve both ruling figures and the distinction between flowing water and rain.
- The printed ruler name has spelling variation within these pages. Record the heading form and retain alternative readings as aliases rather than silently creating different figures.

### 5.6 Miquiztli: death

**Project key:** `miquiztli`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Miquiztli image](../frontend/assets/icons/Miquiztli.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF pages 9–11](scan.pdf#page=9), printed pages 77–80.  
**Source subsections:** *Miquiztli* → *Regente: Tecziztecatl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

What frightened religious chroniclers is presented as part of the Mexica knowledge of life. The first five signs form a sequence:

1. **Cipactli:** what comes first; primordial life; the first light of knowledge.
2. **Ehecatl:** the breath of life, inspiration, and the gift of expression.
3. **Calli:** the security, shelter, and protection people need.
4. **Cuetzpalli:** sexuality as the means of species continuation and pleasure.
5. **Coatl:** energy manifested throughout nature, received and shared by people.

After Coatl comes Miquiztli, death as life's complementary counterpart. It is an inevitable passage for all living beings and a transition toward new forms of life. Death is understood as change, transformation, and a period of rest.

Nature has a quiet interval before life manifests anew. Seeds rest before germinating; the earth rests through winter before being fertilized. In everyday life, Miquiztli is restorative sleep, the concentration preceding creative expression, regeneration before undertaking activity, convalescence, and the interval between menstruation and ovulation through which new beings may be conceived.

Life gives rise to death and death gives rise to life. In this sense Miquiztli and Malinalli, annual grass, are related because both express nature's continual renewal.

#### Regente: Tecziztecatl

**English translation:** In the Teotihuacan narrative, Tecziztecatl is splendidly dressed and proud, but hesitates in fear when ordered to leap into a great fire to become a celestial body. Poorly dressed Nanahuatzin shows courage and leaps first, emerging as the shining Sun, Tonatiuh, who supplies heat, light, and energy to living beings.

Tecziztecatl then leaps too, but is dimmed when a rabbit is thrown at him, leaving its image upon his face. He becomes the Moon or, in the author's interpretation, represents its fertilizing function. His conch shell symbolizes a spiral search, knowledge of life's origin and evolution, and the enduring quality suggested by its hardness.

Metztli is described as his counterpart within the lunar concept. The source associates the Moon with intellect, fluids, tides, and psychic activity at full moon, placing these within its discussion of Tezcatlipoca knowledge.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

The author contrasts European descriptions of the sign as sad, melancholy, or harmful with its account of Indigenous meaning. Those born on a Miquiztli day are assigned reflection, appraisal, and evaluation before acting. Once they begin, they are said to be daring and difficult to stop until they reach their aims.

They are described as disinclined to talk about themselves, introverted, and inwardly closed. Through suggestion, they are said to influence others to transform habits toward what is most appropriate and positive in a given situation.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** death, commonly represented in the project through skeletal imagery.
- **Source themes:** rest; transformation; regeneration; reflection; transition; renewal.
- This source explicitly names **Tecziztecatl** as ruler. Preserve that attribution even if other references use another association.
- The mention of Malinalli establishes a limited cross-reference; it does not replace the missing Malinalli entry.

### 5.14 Ocelotl: jaguar

**Project key:** `ocelotl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Ocelotl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Ocelotl.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF pages 12–13](scan.pdf#page=12), printed pages 98–100; duplicated in [B, PDF pages 7–8](scan0174.pdf#page=7).  
**Source subsections:** *Ocelotl* → *Regente: Tlazohteotl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Ocelotl has been translated as tiger or jaguar. The source describes an agile, slender, beautiful feline, a persistent hunter that approaches cautiously and attacks with force, resolve, and courage. Its yellow-brown coat bears dark bands or rosettes. It also calls the animal *xochimiztli*, “flowered feline,” and interprets each achieved goal or successful hunt as a new flower added to its coat.

The source identifies it as the Olmecs' *nagual*. Within its Tezcatlipoca framework, it carries two apparently opposite meanings. First, its spotted coat evokes the star-filled night sky, a universe close to human sight yet beyond complete understanding. Second, it represents the earth's interior as Tepeyolohtli, Heart of the Mountains, associated with caves and hollows.

Ocelotl also represents the earth-age Ocelotonatiuh, Sun of Jaguar, in the *Leyenda de los soles*. Jaguar warriors and eagle warriors are described as important orders responsible for safeguarding leading people and valuable goods in Anahuac society.

#### Regente: Tlazohteotl

**English translation:** The book presents Tlazohteotl as Mother Earth generating life and providing affection and protection. It explains *tlazohtli* as affection or love and *teotl* as a generating principle.

She is the maiden who, fertilized in spring, becomes the mother of all beings. Earth clothes herself in greenery and many-colored flowers, then fills with fruit to feed and delight her children. She provides the materials people need to live.

After motherhood and full maturity she becomes an elder. Grandmother Earth wears a dry, yellowed skin. Winter arrives with obsidian knives as Itztlacoliuhqui, symbolically bringing death and the interval of winter rest. Annual regeneration returns her to the young maiden who receives cosmic fertilization and becomes mother again.

Her lunar associations appear in her crescent-like nose ornament and Moon imagery on her skirt; cotton bands and spindle ornaments represent femininity in the source's account. Water-mirror imagery on her skirt and a gold-and-turquoise necklace express fertilizing heat associated with Xiuhtecuhtli and the precious blue celestial seed through which the cycle begins again.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Those born on an Ocelotl day are described as striking in appearance, particularly women, cautious in planning, persistent, and determined in execution. They assess their chances of success, capacities, and limitations, and act when confident.

They are credited with social presence and with standing out in a crowd. Some Cipactli qualities recur because Ocelotl is the first sign after the initial sequence of thirteen signs: responsibility and the ability to guide other people.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** jaguar or spotted feline.
- **Source themes:** caution followed by decisive action; accomplishment; night sky; earth's interior; seasonal regeneration; leadership.
- The source shifts between jaguar, tiger, and present-day *ocelote*. Keep that zoological ambiguity visible rather than treating all three as the same modern species.
- The ruler heading is printed **Tlazohteotl**. Preserve this source spelling alongside any separately established project alias.

### 5.15 Cuauhtli: eagle

**Project key:** `cuauhtli`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Cuauhtli image](../frontend/assets/icons/Cuauhtli.png)  
**Source:** [A, PDF page 13](scan.pdf#page=13), printed pages 100–101, continued in [B, PDF page 1](scan0174.pdf#page=1), printed page 102. Also duplicated in B, PDF pages 8–9.  
**Source subsections:** *Cuauhtli* → *Regente: Xipetotec* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

The fifteenth day sign is the eagle, a symbol of high flight, splendor, majestic bearing, and freedom. It inhabits steep mountains, sees prey sharply from great heights, and is daring, at times acting more from impulse than reflection.

The source claims it can face the Sun directly without damaging its sight. Through these qualities it represents the Sun as Tonatiuh's *nagual* at full maturity and in the full manifestation of power and energy.

Eagle plumage forms the insignia of the eagle warriors, warriors of the Sun, ranked alongside jaguar warriors as guardians of society's valued institutions and goods. The source also notes the eagle's place in Mexico's national emblem and its significance among peoples of the Uto-Aztecan linguistic family.

#### Regente: Xipetotec

**English translation:** Xipetotec is explained as “our Lord who changes skin.” Xipe principally represents cyclical changes in nature and changes in human consciousness.

When earth clothes herself in life and greenery, she has cast off the dry, aged skin of winter. An animating energy fills hearts with active restlessness, and complementary opposites are drawn together to preserve species.

Xipe belongs to the source's Red Tezcatlipoca context, containing knowledge of changes in the cosmos and on Mother Earth's surface. The book associates this astronomically with Mixcoatl, the Cloud Serpent, a vaporous band crossing the sky.

Individual and collective disciplines for psychological and physical change occur during annual renewal, *izcalli*. The text connects physical and spiritual cleansing with social membership. Its closing paragraph describes a strict ideal of social order and asserts death penalties for drunkenness, disrespect, theft, and adultery.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

A passage attributed to Durán says Cuauhtli shares the properties of the preceding jaguar sign, with additional qualities. Those born under it are described as persistent, impulsive, and decisive, with keen vision for achieving their aims. They favor high flight and freedom of action in open spaces. Their expression is serious, their bearing splendid and beautiful, particularly for women. They are described as intelligent and resolute.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** an eagle.
- **Source themes:** vision; freedom; solar maturity; decisiveness; renewal; change of skin; transformation.
- The eagle's supposed ability to look safely at the Sun is a source claim, not eye-safety guidance.
- The historical punishment statement is retained as source context, not independently verified legal history.
- The ruler's source spelling is **Xipetotec**; do not assume a spelling variant implies a different figure.

### 5.16 Cozcacuauhtli: vulture / collared bird

**Project key:** `cozcacuauhtli`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Cozcacuauhtli image](../frontend/assets/icons/Cozcacuauhtli.png)  
**Source:** [B, PDF pages 1–2](scan0174.pdf#page=1), printed pages 102–105. Clear duplicates on PDF pages 9 and 11; distorted duplicate on page 10.  
**Source subsections:** *Cozcacuauhtli* → *Regente: Itzpapalotl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

The source translates the name literally as “collared eagle” but says the exact species has not been identified. Chronicles render it as *aura*, vulture, or *buharro*, consistently associating it with a bird of prey.

#### Regente: Itzpapalotl

**English translation:** Itzpapalotl, the obsidian butterfly, is the ruling figure. The text places her as a feminine essence within Dark Tezcatlipoca.

She represents an energy emanating from the earth that affects the feminine essence of beings and opens possibilities for other states of consciousness. She manifests principally at night, from twilight toward dawn. Her influence grows with darkness and fades as dawn's first light appears.

Sunset is a moment for daily self-evaluation. Through reflection, the mind opens to perceptions that flutter like nocturnal butterflies in thought. Itzpapalotl is an essence of Mother Earth that animates nocturnal species and joins a corresponding essence from the starry sky. Wind carries her from ravines and mountains in waves through the darkness.

The source describes her as a key activating dormant genes and neurons, sharpening hearing and vision, and opening other fields of perception, including contact with beings on other levels. It treats *itzpapalotl* as a grade attainable within its account of Tezcatlipoca knowledge, citing Acamapichtli, while saying women generally have greater possibilities of attaining it.

A passage from the Telleriano Codex is described as reporting female forms descending from the upper air and appearing at crossroads and secluded places, which the European commentator called witches. A second passage relates Itzpapalotl, Quetzalcoatl, Tezcatlipoca, and other figures as celestial energies descending to earth. Citlalicue and Citlaltonac are presented as a stellar duality of Ometeotl influencing life through the stars.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

In the account attributed to Durán, people born under this sign are assigned long life, strength, health, tall or substantial bodies, discretion, counsel, and authority. They are described as wise, serious, quiet, prudent, eloquent, inclined to teach, give good advice, and learn.

The author adds that they make the most of opportunities and are skilled at finding practical uses for ideas, objects, tools, and materials others have discarded.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a vulture or collared bird, with exact identification left uncertain by the source.
- **Source themes:** longevity; counsel; learning; practical resourcefulness; night reflection; perception.
- The **bird sign** and **obsidian-butterfly ruling figure** are separate symbolic layers.
- Claims about dormant genes, neurons, other-level beings, and sex-specific capacities remain the source's claims, not established neuroscience or psychology.

### 5.17 Ollin: movement

**Project key:** `ollin`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Ollin image](../frontend/assets/icons/Ollin.png)  
**Source:** [B, PDF pages 2–4](scan0174.pdf#page=2), printed pages 105–108; repeated on PDF pages 11–13.  
**Source subsections:** *Ollin* → *Regente: Xolotl* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Ollin is movement: constant activity in a universe where nothing is static. In nature, activity begins from a center or axis called *olotl*, from which a pulsating vibration puts matter's particles into motion.

In living beings, bodily activity in organs and tissues and mental activity in intellect and subconscious are gathered into *yolli*, the quality of living in constant activity. *Ollin yoliztli*, life and movement, is described as a paired expression revealing duality. *Yolotl* or *yolohtli*, the heart, is the driving organ whose pulsations move the hot liquid of blood and impel decisive action.

The source connects *ollin* or *ullin* with *ulli*, rubber or latex used in decoration and in balls for the ballgame. Rubber is active and vibrating under slight movement. It also associates Ollin with solar vibrations drawn in some manuscripts as a great butterfly, *papalotl*, linking ceaseless motion with thoughts that never remain static.

It describes **Nahui Ollin**, Four Movement, as a day for studying and working with solar phenomena. It says that every 260 days, on this date, the education of wise people and eagle and jaguar warriors culminated in institutions at Tenochtitlan and Malinalco, preparing them to safeguard major social institutions.

#### Regente: Xolotl

**English translation:** Xolotl influences Ollin complementarily, as the companion, complement, or double of something in nature.

The source describes *xolomeh*, young people educated in the *calmecac* to assist leading people with weapons, clothing, and insignia. In adulthood, it says they became *xolotl-quetzalcoatl* and studied Venus's evening-star movements, making continual observations and records in a special observatory called *xoloc*.

It also associates Xolotl's image with ballcourts, which it interprets as astronomical observatories as well as playing spaces. Examples illustrating companionship or doubling include *xoloizcuintli*, the dog accompanying its master to the grave; *xolotl*, a double maize stalk; *mexolotl*, a double-bodied maguey; and *axolotl*, an amphibian in transformation.

Most importantly, Xolotl is the twin of Tlahuizcalpantecuhtli, the morning star: two aspects of one celestial body.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Those born on an Ollin day are described as highly restless and active, continually undertaking demanding tasks with tireless effort. They are excellent, helpful companions, creative, fond of travel, and unable to remain still in one place.

Their inner brightness is said to shine outward and win others' confidence. A passage attributed to Durán calls people born under this sign radiant, fortunate, and blessed with good luck.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** the movement sign; the project's abstract glyph should stay associated with Ollin.
- **Source themes:** activity; pulsation; life; movement; companionship; doubling; creativity; travel.
- Butterfly imagery here expresses motion; it does not make Ollin the same sign as Cozcacuauhtli or its ruler Itzpapalotl.
- Nahui Ollin is a **specific number-sign combination**, not a name for every Ollin day.

### 5.18 Tecpatl: flint knife

**Project key:** `tecpatl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Tecpatl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Tecpatl.png)  
**Source:** [B, PDF pages 4–5](scan0174.pdf#page=4), printed pages 108–110; repeated on PDF pages 13–14.  
**Source subsections:** *Tecpatl* → *Regente: Chalchiuhtotolin* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Tecpatl is translated as a flint knife. The book describes surviving knives made from several hard stones, commonly dark obsidian, used domestically and industrially. Fine, long, thin blades were also used by Mexica surgeons. The specialist term printed in this passage needs a separate Nahuatl transcription check.

Symbolically, Tecpatl is natural or artificial light, such as torchlight. It represents ideas striking from the depths of the mind like sparks from two flints.

The sharply edged knife also represents methodical study that can begin with curiosity. To understand something, one cuts inward from its surface, opening layer after layer until reaching the essence of its structure. The source distinguishes rapidly flying, ungraspable thoughts, associated with the knife-bearing butterfly in the Tezcatlipoca context, from ideas produced through analysis and reflection, which lead to deep knowledge.

Tecpatl also represents winter and intense cold. The text describes wind armed with fine curved obsidian knives that sting and cut the skin.

#### Regente: Chalchiuhtotolin

**English translation:** Chalchiuhtotolin, the jade turkey, rules Tecpatl. The domestic turkey, *huexolotl*, represents both driving force and human vanity.

The source discusses turkey warriors trained to charge with their own strength, bearing shields decorated with feathers earned through conduct in battle. It then describes a small jade turkey awarded to leaders who had overcome vanity and selfishness in order to serve society without exploiting their office.

The principal enemy is within oneself. Knowledge of the inner self and subconscious makes it possible to win an inner struggle. A person who overcomes their own turkey, meaning vanity, can become a torch that illuminates without smoking or a mirror in which others see an example.

The jade turkey bears a smoking-mirror ear ornament in the source's description. In codices it appears with signs of fasting and purification. The book presents fasting as a means of overcoming vanity and selfishness and perhaps opening new fields of perception. It also notes the ceremonial value of turkey feathers among Huichol people.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Tecpatl is mental sharpness that intensifies curiosity and the desire to know origins. Sparks from striking flints are compared to ideas produced through open intuition.

Those born under it are described as studious and deeply curious about the hidden beginnings of things. Once known, they inspire confidence, although their outward appearance may seem hard and cold.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a flint knife or stone blade.
- **Source themes:** illumination; inquiry; analysis; origins; sharpness; self-knowledge; overcoming vanity.
- **Material distinction:** The source's discussion moves between flint and obsidian. Preserve that wording as the source's treatment of stone blades; do not silently treat the materials as geologically identical.
- The turkey is the ruling figure's representation, not the day sign itself.

### 5.19 Quiahuitl: rain

**Project key:** `quiahuitl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Quiahuitl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Quiahuitl.png)  
**Source:** [B, PDF pages 5–6](scan0174.pdf#page=5), printed pages 110–112; duplicated on PDF pages 14–15.  
**Source subsections:** *Quiahuitl* → *Regente: Tonatiuh* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Quiahuitl is rain as a natural manifestation. As an abstract element, the source names *tlaloctli* or Tlaloc, “the liquid the earth drinks.” Rain waters the fields and awakens planted seeds, bringing forth new plants and clothing Mother Earth in green.

It describes early rains as frost in winter and late rains as cooling autumn heat. Rain may arrive as windborne moisture and gentle, rhythmic drops, or violently in hurricane-driven downpours.

In the account of the *tlaloqueh*, beings above the clouds strike great clay vessels. When the vessels break, rain escapes; the fragments collide to produce the electrical flashes of storms.

The sign is described as a face in profile with an eye encircled by a large ring. Two rain signs facing each other produce the face of Tlaloc.

#### Regente: Tonatiuh

**English translation:** Quiahuitl receives the influence of Tonatiuh, who gives heat and energy. As the chief generator of terrestrial life, the Sun is also called Ipalnemohuani, that through which one lives.

The source describes the earth's yearly circuit around the Sun as a *xihuitl*, during which the earth is fertilized and undergoes alternations of greenery and dryness. Equinoxes and solstices mark ceremonies honoring the Sun, with particular emphasis on the winter solstice as the arrival of new energy.

Daily and annual solar energy is represented through changing birds: a hummingbird at dawn, then a macaw, then a *quetzaltototl*, and an eagle at the height of its power. The Sun sets and returns the next day as a turquoise-blue hummingbird.

Those devoted to studying solar movements, changes, and influences are called *quetzalcoatl-Tonatiuh* in the source. Solar influence on plants is called Xochipilli, Child of Flowers, while solar light is represented by Piltzintecuhtli, the Child Lord, whose brushes and paints give nature its colors.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Quiahuitl holds force and passivity at the same level. Those born under it are described as highly imaginative and changeable, shifting readily from sweetness and calm to intense fury.

They are stubborn when pursuing a goal and sweep aside obstacles; their opposite tendency is passivity. The source recommends finding centering elements to keep these attitudes from falling into extremes.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** the rain sign, with Tlaloc-related facial imagery described in the source.
- **Source themes:** rain; germination; nourishment; solar energy; imagination; intensity; balance of extremes.
- This book's ruler heading is **Tonatiuh**, even though the meaning section discusses Tlaloc. The sign's visual association and its ruler field must remain distinct.

### 5.20 Xochitl: flower

**Project key:** `xochitl`  
**Symbol:** [Existing Xochitl image](../frontend/assets/icons/Xochitl.png)  
**Source:** [B, PDF pages 15–16](scan0174.pdf#page=15), printed pages 112–115. The entry opening also appears on PDF page 6.  
**Source subsections:** *Xochitl* → *Regente: Xochiquetzalli* → *Tonalpohualli*.

#### Meaning and symbolism: English translation

Xochitl is the last of the twenty signs used to name the days of the calendar's twenty-day periods. The flower is placed last because it represents the essence of the positive qualities of the other signs.

It symbolizes achievement, a good conclusion, and reaching goals through positive persistence. Xochitl is everything beautiful and excellent in nature. People should cultivate flowering thoughts, express themselves through flowering words, and direct their actions toward flowering.

Artistic creativity in poetry, singing, music, and dance is expressed through **in xochitl in cuicatl**, “flower and song.”

#### Regente: Xochiquetzalli

**English translation:** Xochiquetzalli, the Beautiful Flower, accompanies or rules Xochitl. She comes from the house of breeze and mist where life began on earth, was formed from Omecihuatl's hair, and became the companion of Piltzintecuhtli, the Sun's child.

While he illuminates and colors nature, she takes the hues of the rainbow, *cuzamalotl*, and creates the beauty and visual delight of color that stimulates living beings' spirits. Combining the energies of other natural elements, the pair creates Centeotl, who nourishes and produces plants that feed and heal.

The book cites Ángel María Garibay for Xochiquetzalli's relationship with Tlaloc, with whom she creates harmony, color, and beauty in what fertilizing rain produces. It also cites the *Historia de los mexicanos por sus pinturas*, in which Tezcatlipoca takes her from Tlaloc to the ninth heaven and she becomes the goddess of love.

In the Yauhpan story, her beauty seduces a warrior engaged in meditation and chastity. Tezcatlipoca turns him into a scorpion whose sting transmits the heat prompting the act of love. The author uses the story to discuss the positive and negative possibilities of pleasure, restraint, and the directing of mental and sexual energy toward chosen purposes.

Xochiquetzalli manifests in pleasure, joy, rest, feeling part of nature, and becoming creative. The source describes her influence on women through seductive grace and femininity. It imagines her scattering seeds of love and beauty and sometimes becoming a yellow butterfly fluttering through thought toward beauty and the positive. She carries dance, song, poetry, music, weaving, and all nature's color.

#### Tonalpohualli interpretation: English translation

Xochitl is everything beautiful and flowering that the human mind can generate. Those associated with it are described as artistically creative and persistent in achieving a chosen aim. Clear in speech, pure in spirit, and firm in will, they flower so that they can bear fruit.

The section ends with a Nahuatl verse and Spanish rendering credited to **Cantares mexicanos, folio 36 recto**. An English rendering of the printed Spanish is:

> Let the singer sing,
> the one who bears the shield of sunlight.
> Like the rainbow, I see your flowers.
> My heart rejoices, for I know
> the precious stones.

This is a translation of the book's Spanish rendering, **not a newly established translation directly from the Nahuatl**.

#### Symbol association and editorial notes

- **Image subject:** a flower.
- **Source themes:** beauty; artistic creation; song; poetry; color; pleasure; achievement; flowering; fruitfulness.
- The source heading is **Xochiquetzalli**. Preserve that spelling and keep it distinguishable from differently spelled names in other references until a deliberate editorial normalization.
- “Flower and song” is a paired expression for artistic creativity in this passage; it is more than an instruction to display a flower icon.

## 6. Patterns across the source

This section is **editorial synthesis from the translations above**, not an additional translated chapter.

### 6.1 The day-sign entry has three distinct layers

| Layer | What it answers | Example | Why separation matters |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sign and symbol | What is the sign, and what imagery does it carry? | Calli: a house, containment, shelter | Identifies the symbol itself |
| Regente | Which figure or function does this source associate with the sign? | Calli: Tepeyolohtli, represented as a jaguar | A ruler's imagery can differ from the sign's image |
| Tonalpohualli interpretation | What human qualities does the source attribute to birth under the sign? | Calli: protective, home-loving, planning ahead | An attributed character reading has a different status from the symbol's name |

The Quiahuitl entry makes this especially clear: its image description invokes Tlaloc, but its **Regente heading names Tonatiuh**. Combining those fields would erase a distinction made by the source.

### 6.2 The number sequence follows a developmental argument

The numbers do not read as thirteen disconnected keywords. The source builds a progression:

1. **1–4: formation.** A unified whole acquires structure, circulating vitality, and embodied form within cosmic order.
2. **5: ability.** Hands and fingers give that formed being the power to make and appreciate beauty.
3. **6–8: inner work.** Introspection, intellectual activity, and the growth of inner essence develop the person.
4. **9–10: orientation and cooperation.** Centrality is followed by the paired hands' balance and complementary skill.
5. **11–12: expression and clarity.** A pause for grace and enjoyment precedes inner transparency.
6. **13: synthesis and fruitfulness.** The earlier qualities join in wisdom, interdependence, and the capacity to bear fruit.

The labels “formation,” “ability,” and “inner work” are editorial groupings. The supporting ideas are in the numbered passages.

### 6.3 The first six signs also form an explicit sequence

The book itself summarizes this sequence under Miquiztli, printed page 78:

| Sign | Place in the book's sequence |
| --- | --- |
| Cipactli | Primordial life and first knowledge |
| Ehecatl | Breath, inspiration, expression |
| Calli | Shelter and protection |
| Cuetzpalli | Sexuality, continuation of species, pleasure |
| Coatl | Life energy received and shared |
| Miquiztli | Rest, transformation, and renewed life |

This is stronger evidence for a connected pattern than assigning an invented twenty-stage story. The missing middle pages prevent checking whether the source extends a similarly explicit argument through all twenty entries.

### 6.4 Recurring themes connect numbers with signs

These are **thematic cross-references**, not equivalences, historical deity assignments, or ready-made combined-date predictions.

| Recurring theme | Numbers expressing it | Signs expressing it in surviving text |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Origin and beginnings | 1 | Cipactli |
| Duality and complementarity | 2, 10 | Coatl; paired Cipactli rulers; Xolotl under Ollin |
| Life-bearing water and warmth | 3, 4 | Coatl; Quiahuitl; Ehecatl's clouds and breath |
| Form, protection, containment | 2, 4, 8 | Calli |
| Making and artistic expression | 5, 11 | Ehecatl; Xochitl |
| Introspection and deep knowledge | 6, 7, 8 | Calli; Miquiztli; Acatl fragment; Tecpatl |
| Rest as part of transformation | 11 | Miquiztli; winter imagery under Ocelotl |
| Centrality and balance | 5, 9, 10 | Quiahuitl's need to center extremes; Acatl's centered observation |
| Flowering, completion, fruit | 12, 13 | Xochitl; Ocelotl's flowered coat and achieved aims |
| Movement and active life | 7; also the activity described under 4 | Ollin; Cuauhtli; Mazatl fragment |

For example, **5 Xochitl** could place Five's making and perception beside Xochitl's artistic flowering. The PDFs do **not** supply a specific combined reading for 5 Xochitl in these sections. Such a sentence would be a new synthesis and must be labeled as such.

### 6.5 Nature and human conduct mirror each other

The text repeatedly moves from a natural process to a human analogy:

- Wind carries sound → expression, inspiration, singing, and artistic ideas.
- A house contains and protects → inward knowledge, family, and provision.
- Death and winter rest precede renewal → reflection before activity and recovery.
- A jaguar stalks, then strikes → cautious planning followed by determined action.
- An eagle sees from above → sharp vision, ambition, and freedom of action.
- Flint cuts into layers and produces sparks → inquiry, analysis, and ideas.
- Rain ranges from gentle drops to storms → imagination, calm, force, and changing intensity.
- A flower blooms and bears fruit → beauty, accomplishment, creativity, and mature results.

These analogies explain the source's structure. They do not prove the corresponding birth-character claims.

### 6.6 The same image can serve different concepts

- **Jaguar:** Ocelotl's sign; Tepeyolohtli under Calli; earth's interior; the starry sky.
- **Butterfly:** symmetry under Two; solar activity under Ollin; Itzpapalotl under Cozcacuauhtli; beauty and creativity under Xochitl.
- **Serpent:** Coatl's sign; undulating life energy in Quetzalcoatl; paired forces and fertility.
- **Birds:** Mazatl is linked with solar animals including the hummingbird; Cuauhtli represents solar maturity; Quiahuitl's Tonatiuh discussion uses several birds for solar phases.
- **Water:** Three's bodily and natural fluids; Coatl's flowing water; Quiahuitl's rain. The source distinguishes these functions.

A shared motif is not enough to merge entries or substitute one image for another.

### 6.7 Colonial interpretations are repeatedly challenged

The author disputes negative chronicler readings of wind, sexuality, serpents, and death, proposing life-sustaining or regenerative meanings instead. This is part of the book's interpretive position.

At the same time, it relies on passages attributed to Durán, Sahagún's informants, and codices. A future presentation should distinguish the book author's argument from the historical passages being quoted or paraphrased. These scans do not provide full references for checking all of those passages.

## 9. Items to resolve before incorporation

1. **Recover printed pages 81–96.** They contain Mazatl's continuation, the absent middle entries, and Acatl's opening. The current PDFs cannot supply those translations.
2. **Identify the edition.** A title page and publication details are needed for a full citation of *Calendario Mexicano*.
3. **Choose translation depth.** This file provides detailed condensed translations. A publication-grade bilingual edition would require line-level Spanish transcription, a complete translation, and language review, particularly of Nahuatl forms and etymologies.
4. **Keep sources separate.** The book's ruling figures and character readings should not overwrite the existing source-specific records without a deliberate editorial decision.
5. **Decide how to present birth-character readings.** They can be labeled as this book's interpretation; they should remain distinct from literal sign meanings and historical descriptions.
6. **Review asserted etymologies and scientific language before public copy.** Preserve them in research notes, but independently assess them before presenting them as factual explanations.
7. **Review spelling against the book and the project's language policy.** Stable entity IDs allow source spellings to coexist without changing the calendar's identity mapping.
8. **Avoid fabricating combined-date meanings.** The number and sign descriptions can be compared, but these scans do not provide 260 individually translated date readings.
